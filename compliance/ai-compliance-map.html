AI compliance map

Where each thing Tessera's AI does in the prototype meets the EU AI Act and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework: what is already built, what is partial, and what is still open.

Grounding, not legal advice. Tessera is fictional. This map is a design tool for a portfolio: it shows how the product's human-oversight, explanation and audit features trace back to real frameworks. Article and subcategory references are to the EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) and NIST AI RMF 1.0. Application dates for parts of the EU Act have been under revision, so check current dates before relying on any of this.

What the map found

Two directions of compliance are in play. This page covers the first: the rules Tessera's own AI has to meet. The second, where the AI helps customers meet DORA, NIS2 or the EU AI Act for their vendors, shows up here only where a wrong AI answer would carry a regulatory cost (regulatory calls, evidence review).

Good news

Vendor scoring is probably not high-risk

Annex III lists uses such as employment, credit scoring of people, and essential services. Scoring companies for vendor risk is not on the list. Tessera still designs to the high-risk bar for human oversight (Arts. 12 to 15), which is the differentiator.

Decision needed

The team score is the exception

The Team block scores each analyst and the chat coaches on it. Evaluating people at work is an Annex III(4)(b) use. Either show team-level measures only, or build it as high-risk, with notice to workers (Art. 26(7)).

Strongest

Human oversight is built

Chat proposals wait for approval. Regulatory calls need a reason to override. Evidence exceptions cite their page. That covers most of Art. 14(4) and NIST GOVERN 3.2 and MAP 3.5.

Biggest gap

No record you can look at

Approvals and overrides are captured, but there is no history view (Art. 12, MANAGE 4.1), and no way to say "this signal or exception is wrong" (MEASURE 3.3).

Also touching the AI

Frameworks from the reference list that apply to Tessera's own AI, beyond the two above.

GDPR

Breach dumps and paste sites hold personal data, so the scan should keep the vendor-attributable indicator and not the records (Art. 5(1)(c)). Art. 22 is unlikely to apply because a person confirms each decision, and the decisions concern companies. Analyst scoring brings employee-data rules (Art. 88) with it.

OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

Three risks bite here. Prompt injection: vendor documents are untrusted input to the evidence reader. Excessive agency: the approval step in every proposal is the control. Overreliance: confidence, citations and mandatory reasons push against it.

MITRE ATLAS

The scan is an attack surface. An adversary could plant false signals to make a vendor look worse (or better). Signal source, corroboration and the confidence figure are the defences to show.

ISO/IEC 42001

The management-system standard is how the "outside the UI" rows would be evidenced: risk process, data governance, documentation, testing. Worth citing when a customer asks for proof.

Canada, AIDA

The reference list calls it proposed. Bill C-27 lapsed in January 2025 and no federal AI statute is in force. Canadian customers still expect the same oversight, so the design does not change, but the list should say "lapsed".

US state law

Colorado's AI Act and similar laws focus on consequential decisions about people. That again points at the analyst-scoring feature, not at vendor scoring.

What to design next

Ordered by how much of the map each one closes.

  1. An activity log. A single history of what the AI proposed, what a person approved, declined or overrode, and why. Closes Art. 12 and MANAGE 4.1, and gives the override-rate signal.Fits the unbuilt Remediation & Tasks section, or a History tab in the task drawer.
  2. "This is wrong" on every AI output. A thumbs-down that opens a short reason on signals, evidence exceptions, triage calls and chat answers. Closes MEASURE 3.3 and the overreliance risk.
  3. Decide the team score. Show team-level measures only, or design the individual score as high-risk: notice to analysts, a way to contest it, and a human review step.The first is the smaller change and removes the Annex III(4) question.
  4. Why this score. A breakdown on the Portfolio Risk Score card itself, not only in the chat, showing tier weights, age and the top contributors. Closes Art. 13 and MEASURE 2.9 for the headline number.
  5. About Tessera AI. One page: intended purpose, what it cannot do, accuracy, and how to override. Linked from the chat footer. Closes Art. 13, Art. 4, MAP 1.1 and MAP 2.2.
  6. Guard the standing rule. Auto-assign needs an owner, a review date and a log of what it did, so a switched-on rule does not become invisible.Closes MANAGE 2.4.
